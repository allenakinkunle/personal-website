Many teams already have software for every named part of the business. They have systems for accounting, projects, documents, contracts, and communication. The persistent difficulty is the work that happens between them.

That work appears as a weekly reconciliation, a status meeting, a folder of exported files, or a message asking which number is current. It is easy to dismiss these routines as administrative overhead. In practice, they are where the organisation rebuilds a shared version of reality.

Designing for this space changes what matters. Integration is only the beginning. Two systems can exchange data perfectly while the people using them still disagree about meaning. A useful product needs to preserve context: when a value changed, who reviewed it, what assumption sits behind it, and whether it is ready to be acted on.

Exceptions matter more than the happy path. The routine case is often already automated or understood. Trust is earned when something arrives late, totals do not match, or responsibility is unclear. The interface should make those moments calm enough to resolve rather than hiding them behind a red badge.

The work between systems is not empty space. It is a product surface with its own users, rules, and consequences. Treating it that way is often the first step toward making an operation feel like one coherent system rather than a collection of tools.
