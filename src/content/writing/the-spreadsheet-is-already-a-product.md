When a spreadsheet becomes important enough, people stop describing it as a spreadsheet. It becomes the tracker, the forecast, the handover, or simply the place where the truth lives. The language changes because the object has quietly become infrastructure.

That makes it a useful place to begin product discovery. A mature operational spreadsheet contains more than rows and formulas. It holds decisions about who is responsible, which exceptions matter, what gets reviewed, and how uncertainty is handled. Its awkwardness often records the shape of the work more faithfully than a polished requirements document.

The temptation is to rebuild it screen for screen. That usually preserves the clutter while removing the flexibility that made the spreadsheet useful. A better approach is to ask what job each column is doing. Some columns store facts. Others signal risk, coordinate a handoff, or remind someone to make a judgment. Those are different product responsibilities and deserve different interactions.

Good operational software does not merely digitise the sheet. It makes the underlying system legible. It should show where information came from, make ownership obvious, and give exceptions enough room to remain exceptions. The product succeeds when the team can spend less time maintaining its representation of the work and more time doing the work itself.

The spreadsheet was never the enemy. It was the first prototype, built by the people closest to the problem.
